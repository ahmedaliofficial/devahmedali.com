Model selection usually happens once. Somebody evaluates the options at the start of a project, picks the strongest available, and every call in the system goes to it from then on. The decision is treated as a platform choice, like picking a database, and it inherits that sense of permanence.

It is closer to choosing an instance size. A system doing anything substantial has several distinct jobs inside it, and those jobs have genuinely different requirements: some need careful reasoning, most need speed and consistency, a few need neither and are being asked to classify a sentence. Routing all of them to the most capable model is the equivalent of running every workload on the largest machine in the fleet.

## The jobs inside one feature are not alike

A retrieval-backed assistant contains at least four model calls with different profiles.

Query understanding, turning a user's phrasing into something searchable, is short, high-volume, latency-critical and not difficult. Classification and routing, deciding which path a request takes, is the same. Both are ideal for a small fast model, and both are frequently sent to a frontier one because that is what the client was configured with.

Answer generation over retrieved context is where capability earns its price, because the failures there are visible to the user and the reasoning genuinely matters.

Evaluation, where [a model grades output](/blog/llm-as-judge-and-when-to-trust-it), wants something capable and, more importantly, something stable, since a judge that changes is a measurement instrument that changes.

Treating those as one decision means either overpaying on three of them or underperforming on one.

## Three criteria that matter more than a benchmark

**Latency at the position in the pipeline.** A step in the critical path of an interactive response has a budget measured in hundreds of milliseconds. A background enrichment job does not. Benchmark accuracy tells you nothing about whether the interface will feel responsive, and users notice latency more reliably than they notice a marginal quality difference.

**Stability of behaviour over the deprecation cycle.** How long the version is supported, what the migration path looks like, and how much your prompts depend on its specific quirks. A model that is slightly better and deprecated in six months is a worse choice for a system you intend to keep.

**Cost at the volume that step will actually run at.** A query-understanding call fires on every request, so its unit cost is multiplied by total traffic. An answer-generation call fires once per question. The same price difference has a very different consequence in those two positions.

## Where I would spend the capability

Put the best model where the output is read by a person and the reasoning is hard. Put a small fast one everywhere the task is mechanical: routing, extraction, [structured output against a schema](/blog/structured-output-you-can-parse), query rewriting. That split has consistently cut both cost and latency without a measurable quality change, and it is the first optimisation I would look for in an existing system.

The thing that makes this safe rather than risky is having [an eval set per step](/blog/why-evals-come-before-scale). Swapping a model on the routing call is a ten-minute experiment when there is a suite that tells you whether routing got worse. Without one it is a change nobody can evaluate, which is why teams avoid making it and keep paying for capability they are not using.

## A per-call decision, behind one interface

The practical arrangement is a thin layer where each logical step names the model it wants, rather than a single client configured once and imported everywhere. It costs almost nothing to build at the start and it is what makes the per-step choice available at all.

The models will change. The right one for each step will change independently, at different times, for different reasons. A system where that is a configuration line per step absorbs the churn. One where the model is assumed throughout absorbs it as a project, every time.
